**AI Builders Digest — June 18, 2026**

Two things happened this week that look unrelated but aren't: Midjourney announced a full-body ultrasound scanner, and OpenAI published results from an AI that independently improved a drug-making reaction. The AI health story is no longer "chatbots that read your lab results." It's moving toward machines that generate the data and discover the chemistry in the first place.

---

**Midjourney wants to scan your organs the way you step on a scale**

Midjourney, the AI image company that famously never took venture money, announced its second product: a full-body ultrasonic CT scanner. The device uses 358,000 ultrasonic elements arranged in a ring, captures roughly 40 GB of data per body slice, and reconstructs images using 21 servers. Founder David Holz called it "the first new whole-body medical imaging modality in 50 years." Notably, there's no AI in the scanner itself yet. The hardware comes first; the models come later, once there's data to train on.

Why it matters: The play here is the data. Every MRI and CT scan taken today goes into a hospital's system. If Midjourney can put a scanner in a wellness clinic or a spa and get millions of people scanning voluntarily, they'd own a body-imaging dataset that no hospital network has. That's not a healthcare product launch. That's a data acquisition strategy.

**An AI chemist improved a drug reaction on its own. OpenAI wants credit.**

OpenAI and biotech startup Molecule.one published results showing that an agent built on GPT-5.4 autonomously improved a key reaction step in medicinal chemistry, the kind of work that normally takes a PhD chemist months of lab iteration. "Near-autonomous" is doing some work in that headline, but the underlying claim is that the agent proposed, evaluated, and refined experimental conditions without a human directing each step.

Why it matters: If this scales past one curated demo, it rewrites the drug discovery timeline. The bottleneck in early-stage pharma isn't funding or ideas. It's the number of experiments a small team can physically run. An agent that can shoulder even 20% of that iteration work compresses years into months. Watch for Molecule.one's next funding round.

**Peter Yang is teaching Codex and Claude Code to give you personalized advice**

Product manager Peter Yang posted that he's releasing a tutorial on turning Codex or Claude Code into a personalized advisor using a four-file skill setup. He also mentioned saving advice from Fable, an AI tool that recently restricted access, before it went dark.

Why it matters: This is a quick hit, but the pattern is worth noting. "Save the AI's advice before it disappears" is now a real workflow concern. People are building institutional knowledge inside specific AI tools, and when those tools change access or shut down, that knowledge evaporates. Your team's best prompts and configurations deserve a backup strategy.

**Slack now renders HTML attachments instead of showing raw code**

Developer Thariq posted that Slack has quietly shipped HTML attachment rendering, meaning formatted output from AI tools now displays as actual formatted content instead of a wall of angle brackets.

This is the kind of change that sounds minor until you realize how many AI-generated reports and summaries have been landing in Slack as unreadable markup for the past two years. Small fix, real improvement.
