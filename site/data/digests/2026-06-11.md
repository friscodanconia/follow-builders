**The real problem with AI coding tools isn't the interface**
Zara Zhang pointed out something most companies miss: non-technical people don't struggle with chat interfaces for coding agents. They struggle with knowing what to ask for. The blank chat box assumes you already understand what's possible. Zhang was impressed by Town's onboarding, which proactively suggests workflows instead of waiting for users to figure out what to request.
Why it matters: Every company building "natural language" coding tools is solving the wrong problem. The hard part isn't making AI understand English — it's helping humans understand what they can build.

**Anthropic engineer reveals the secret to long-running AI workflows**
Boris Cherny from Anthropic explained why self-verification loops matter for AI models that run for extended periods. When models can check their own work automatically, they deliver results closer to what you intended without constant human supervision. The technique becomes essential as models get powerful enough to work autonomously for hours, not minutes.
Why it matters: The difference between an AI tool and an AI coworker is whether it can catch its own mistakes. Companies that figure out verification loops first will have agents that actually work unsupervised.
https://x.com/bcherny/status/2064426115255730578

**Swyx discovers Anthropic's Fable is a code review game-changer**
AI researcher Swyx reported that telling Claude Code to "review my code for issues" on Anthropic's Fable platform produces remarkably good results. He suggested developers will feel horrified about shipping code to production without running it through Fable first. The platform currently doesn't charge per use.
Why it matters: If Anthropic's Fable can catch bugs that human reviewers miss, code review just became an AI-first workflow. Every engineering team will need to decide whether their human reviewers are still worth the bottleneck.

**Sarah Guo's framework explains why some AI companies will win**
Latent Space Newsletter highlighted venture capitalist Sarah Guo's analysis of which AI companies have lasting advantages. Her key insight: successful AI applications earn their place by doing "unglamorous work" — integrating with a company's private systems and changing how people actually work. The integration and maintenance never end, making these companies hard to replace.
Why it matters: Every AI startup pitching "we built a better model" is missing the point. The winners will be the ones willing to do the messy work of making AI fit into how businesses actually operate.
