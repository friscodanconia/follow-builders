**Anthropic puts its frontier model to work defending security teams**

Claude's official account confirmed that Anthropic is integrating Mythos 5, its highest-capability model tier, directly into partner security products and services. Alongside that, Anthropic launched a Defender Advantage Fund with $35M in credits for open-source security projects and is expanding its Cyber Verification Program in the coming weeks.

Why it matters: Anthropic has been careful to position its most powerful models as enterprise-grade, privacy-first infrastructure. Now it's making a direct pitch to security teams: the same model that previously required careful enterprise deployment is becoming a product your SOC can actually buy. If your company uses third-party security tooling, there's a real chance Mythos 5 is in its next major release.

**OpenAI's Codex is eating users' quotas faster than it should**

Thibault Sottiaux, who works on Codex at OpenAI, posted a public update acknowledging that cache hit rates have degraded for some users this week, meaning Codex is processing more work from scratch instead of reusing prior results. The practical effect: usage limits drain faster than expected, leaving developers hitting walls mid-session. An update was promised for the following day.

Why it matters: Codex's value proposition depends on being fast and quota-efficient. When the cache breaks, it stops being a power tool and starts being an expensive one. The 539 replies on this post tell you how many developers noticed. If your team builds on Codex and this week felt slower than usual, now you know why.

**An AI email app is holding users' data hostage**

Product builder Peter Yang called out Instinct, an AI email assistant, for indexing and retaining user emails without clear permission and then refusing to allow deletion of that data. Yang said he can't recommend the product until it's resolved.

Why it matters: This is the consent problem with AI productivity apps in plain view. When an app's core feature is "read all your email and make it searchable," the data retention policy is not a footnote. It's the whole product. Any AI tool that touches your inbox deserves the same scrutiny you'd apply to a new hire with access to your Outlook. If you've connected an AI assistant to your email recently, it's worth checking what their deletion policy actually says.

**Swyx on why "simulation as a scaling law" stopped sounding like hype**

Swyx, who writes and podcasts extensively on AI, posted about a shift in his own thinking after recording an interview on AI simulation research. He admitted he went into the conversation skeptical, treating "simulation is a new scaling law" as a marketing line, and came out treating it as one of the more important ideas in AI development. The thread traces the logic from early academic work like the Smallville project through to recursive self-improvement, where AI systems increasingly automate parts of their own research and engineering.

Why it matters: Swyx is a reliable signal-to-noise filter for what's actually worth tracking in AI research. When he publicly updates his priors in writing, it's usually worth noting. The specific claim here, that simulation could matter as much to the next generation of AI as raw compute scaling did to the last, is one to watch. It's not confirmed. But the people who backed it early include names that have been right before.
