**AI Builders Digest — June 28, 2026**

The frontier model access debate just got a plot twist. As labs talk about gating who can use their best models, Box CEO Aaron Levie is out here saying GPT-5.6 is real and strong. The tension between "share everything" and "lock it down" is shaping up to be the defining fight of the next 12 months, and most companies haven't picked a side yet.

---

**GPT-5.6 is real, and it's built for agents that actually do things**

Box CEO Aaron Levie confirmed GPT-5.6 exists and called it "very strong" for knowledge worker tasks involving heavy tool use and long-running agents. He also pushed back on the plateau narrative directly: "We're not hitting any walls in AI progress right now."

Why it matters: If GPT-5.6 lands the way Levie describes, the next wave of enterprise automation isn't about chatbots answering questions. It's about agents that run multi-step workflows without a human in the loop. Your company's legal review, invoice processing, or vendor onboarding is a candidate. The only question is whether your data is clean enough to let an agent touch it.

**The open-source distillation loop is breaking the frontier model business model**

Peter Yang, a product creator and writer, laid out the uncomfortable cycle that keeps playing out: labs publish powerful frontier models, competitors distill them into cheap open-source alternatives, US companies adopt those alternatives because they're good enough and cheaper, and then labs respond by gating access to their frontier models. Yang's question: does this end with US companies innovating less, while open-source keeps getting more attractive?

Why it matters: Yesterday we covered identity verification coming to model access. Today you can see why: if every frontier model release creates a cheaper open-source version within months, the labs have almost no window to monetize their best work. Gating access is their answer. But Yang's scenario plays out badly for anyone building products on top of those gated models. You're one policy change away from a pricing shock.

**Claude Code on desktop ships split-screen, and it's more useful than it sounds**

Cat Wu, who works on Claude Code at Anthropic, highlighted split-screen as one of her favorite features in the desktop app. The demo shows code and output side by side without juggling windows.

Why it matters: The developers who move fastest aren't necessarily using the smartest model. They're using the one with the least friction. Split-screen in a native desktop app is the kind of detail that sounds minor until you've spent a week switching between terminal, editor, and chat window.

**AI is making some builders impatient with small talk**

Aditya Agarwal, a technology executive, shared a personal observation that's been circulating in developer circles: spending all day with AI agents that respond instantly and precisely is changing his tolerance for shallow human interactions. He wants more depth in relationships, and agents to handle everything else. He predicts the world becomes "both smaller and more rich in its relationship depth."

Why it matters: This is a real behavioral shift worth watching, not just a personal quirk. If the people building AI tools are already recalibrating what they expect from human interaction, think about what happens when these tools reach a much larger population. The social friction that comes from that adjustment won't show up in any benchmark.
